Did the bus network decide who lost metro riders when the Yellow Line opened?

A natural experiment that joins BMRCL hourly ridership with dated BMTC bus-network snapshots. It runs on open data you can download today, processed in Spark on HDFS.

Yellow Line opened 11 Aug 2025 83 metro stations · ~5,000 bus routes ~1.2M origin–destination rows Build deadline: end of 22 Nov 2026

The idea

Before 11 Aug 2025 there was no metro to Hosur Road or Electronic City. Many commuters heading there rode the Purple or Green Line to a station such as Banashankari or Chickpete, got off, and caught a BMTC bus for the rest of the trip.

When the Yellow Line opened, those commuters could stay in the metro: change at RV Road and ride straight to a Yellow station. In the metro data, their exit station changes. The same origin sends fewer people to the old bus-transfer station and more to Yellow stations.

Green/Purple LineYellow LineBMTC bus legBold label = exit station recorded at the fare gate
Research question: when a new metro line opens, does the existing bus network predict how ridership is redistributed across the old metro stations?

Why it needs both datasets. Metro data alone doesn't explain why a station mattered. Bus data alone doesn't show that metro riders moved. Combining them turns "bus + metro" into a question that can be tested.

Method

  1. Spark pipeline on HDFS

    Load the parquet files and the GTFS text files into HDFS, harmonise station names using the repo's name and code maps, and join each metro station to the BMTC stops within 400 m and 800 m, measured along the OSM walking network.

  2. Bus-link score for each old station

    From the 4 Aug bus network: YellowLink_s is the share of bus trips near station s that also stop within 800 m of a Yellow station. Also compute BusTrips_s and Collinear_s (bus route length running alongside the Yellow alignment). Freeze the top-10 "bus-transfer stations" and pre-register the list and the main specification on OSF before looking at ridership.

  3. Main result

    Origin–destination diversion test

    For each origin, did the share of trips going to bus-transfer stations fall after 11 Aug? Comparing within the same origin on the same day cancels anything local to that origin, such as the flower show, joyriders or rain.

    log(1+flow[o,d,day,hr]) ~ FE[o×day×hr] + FE[o×d×hr]
                          + β · After × YellowLink_d      (expect β < 0)

    Estimate it with log-OLS and with PPML (Poisson pseudo-maximum likelihood), since many station pairs have zero trips in a given hour.

    Cross-check: origins that lost the most trips to bus-transfer stations should be the same origins that gained the most trips to Yellow stations.

  4. Time-of-day signature

    Rerouted commuters should mean fewer morning-peak exits and fewer evening-peak entries at bus-transfer stations. The flower show would add midday and weekend traffic instead.

  5. September persistence

    Check whether the drop holds across 30 days of entries. Drop the 6–7 am hour, because first service moved to 6:00 am on 10 Sep. Then estimate the headway elasticity: an event study of Yellow-station entries around the 10 Sep cut from 25 to 19 min, with a synthetic-control check, split by whether a station gained BMTC feeder routes.

  6. How BMTC responded

    Diff the 4 Aug and 2 Sep bus networks: trips cut on old links to Hosur Road, and feeder routes added at Yellow stations. Then track daily bus trips near Yellow stations vs other stations across all 27 GTFS snapshots (Feb 2024 to Nov 2026), as a supply-side event study.

  7. Robustness

    Correlation of the score with distance to RV Road, plus the main test within a single line. Placebo opening dates (5 and 7 Aug). Shuffling the score across stations 1,000 times. Wild-cluster bootstrap for standard errors. Dropping 15–16 Aug, Lalbagh and the first two days of joyriding. 400 m vs 800 m buffers. The score recomputed from the 2 Sep snapshot.

  8. Spark scaling

    Time the pipeline on 1, 2 and 4 executors and at 1×, 5× and 20× data volume, and compare against the same job in pandas on one machine.

  9. Model comparison

    Spark MLlib: does bus data improve prediction?

    Predict hourly station entries in the week after the opening (11–18 Aug). Train on 11–15 Aug and test on 16–18 Aug. Fit Linear Regression, Random Forest and Gradient-Boosted Trees, each twice: with metro-only features (station, hour, weekday, pre-opening average, distance to RV Road) and with metro + bus features (BusTrips_s, YellowLink_s). Report RMSE, MAE and R². If the bus features help, that is the case for combining the datasets, stated as a number. If they don't, report that honestly.

Two-day build plan

Every step stays in. Three people run notebooks in parallel on Kaggle and Colab: A on the pipeline and scaling, B on the bus network and maps, C on ridership analysis and statistics.

Compute setup

  • Kaggle notebook (4 CPU cores, ~30 GB RAM): Hadoop single-node HDFS + PySpark, the OSM walking network, the MLlib models and the scaling runs on local[1], local[2] and local[4].
  • Colab (2 cores, ~12 GB RAM): runs in parallel for the 1,000-permutation test, the bootstrap, the regressions and the figures.
  • Shared data: upload all raw files once as a private Kaggle Dataset (metro parquet + 27 GTFS zips, ~3 GB) so every notebook reads the same copy. Save outputs to the Kaggle output folder or Google Drive.
  • HDFS tip: start daemons with hdfs --daemon start namenode and hdfs --daemon start datanode instead of start-dfs.sh, which needs SSH and fails on Colab and Kaggle.

Is it publishable?

What's new: the first quasi-experimental study of an Indian metro opening that uses the dated bus network to test why ridership moved. Hasan et al. 2025 (TR-A) is a single snapshot with no opening event. Gadepalli et al. 2022 is descriptive and uses closed data. The Diagram Chasing blog only guessed this explanation.
